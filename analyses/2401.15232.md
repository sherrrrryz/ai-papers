# How Beginning Programmers and Code LLMs (Mis)read Each Other

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · Verifying AI analysis · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
A controlled lab study with 120 students (40 each from Northeastern, Oberlin and Wellesley) who had finished CS1 and nothing beyond it. Participants saw a function signature plus input/output examples and had to describe the function in natural language to "Charlie the Coding Cow", a Codex-based tool that generated code and ran tests automatically. They could not edit the code, only retry the prompt. Each person did 8 problems (4 untimed, 4 timed), and after each one said whether the code was correct.

## Key findings
- Students solved 4.7 of 8 problems on average; per-attempt success was only 24%, eventual success 57%, and they did not get better at prompting across the 75-minute session.
- Background mattered: CS1-only students had pass@1 of 0.17 vs 0.24 for those with more experience (p = 0.02); first-generation students 0.17 vs 0.23 (p = 0.04).
- Students judged correctness mostly from the test results shown, not from reading the code: 61.8% said the code was correct, and 91% of those were cases where all tests passed. Unfamiliar constructs (lambda, map, list comprehensions) blocked comprehension.
- Students often could not tell why a prompt failed; identical prompts producing different code (4.2% of cases) was read as their own failure. The most common mental model was a keyword lookup (46 students); only 19 described it as an LLM.

## Why it relates to your idea
This is evidence for the core problem in your proposal: non-experts struggle at each step of stating intent, judging output and repairing it, and the ability to judge depends on technical background. It sits alongside your Gu et al. and "Not Everyone Wins" anchors. It also bears on RQ1: your hypothesis card asks a non-coder to judge an inference drawn from a code diff, which is the same verification gap.

## What you can take from it
- Cite it in the motivation as a large-N (120) demonstration that novices verify AI code by external signals (passing tests), not by understanding. Your design gives designers an external signal they can read: events firing live in the prototype.
- Do not ask participants to read the diff or the instrumentation code. Translate into the prototype vocabulary they already have, since unfamiliar constructs were a key blocker.
- Borrow the post-task item "is this output correct?" for your comprehension test, and compare self-judged correctness against actual correctness to measure miscalibration, as this paper did.
- Its finding that the AI's stochasticity was misread as user failure supports labelling AI-written hypotheses and captions as AI-generated and regenerable.

## Limits for your use
- Participants were CS students writing code, not designers or PMs judging experiment plans.
- Automated tests did the judging for them, so the paper says little about unaided verification.
- Single older model (Codex), selective US institutions.
