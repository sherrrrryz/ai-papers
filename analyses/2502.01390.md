# Plan-Then-Execute: An Empirical Study of User Trust and Team Performance When Using LLM Agents As A Daily Assistant

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · RQ1 Planning · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
A 2×2 between-subjects crowdsourcing study (CHI 2025) with N = 248 Prolific workers. A GPT-3.5 plan-and-execute agent carried out six everyday tasks (for example, flight booking, credit card repayment) against simulated APIs. The conditions varied whether users could edit the agent's plan (add, delete, split, edit steps) and whether they approved, corrected or overrode each action during execution. Three tasks were high-risk and some initial plans were deliberately flawed.

## Key findings
- Users often trusted flawed plans. Calibrated trust in planning averaged 0.50, and it was lowest on high-risk tasks, where all initial plans were imperfect.
- Involvement did not help across the board. The hypotheses were not supported in general, and effects appeared only on specific tasks.
- Plan editing hurt when the plan was already correct. On task 4, action-sequence accuracy fell from 0.95 to 0.82. It helped when the plan had a visible error (plan quality 2.0 to 2.4 on task 1).
- Execution involvement raised accuracy on task 6 (about 0.05 to 0.22) but lowered it on task 3 (0.44 to 0.12).
- Plan involvement raised mental demand, temporal demand and frustration on the NASA-TLX. Subjective trust did not change (about 3.3 to 3.6 out of 5).

## Why it relates to your idea
Your page is a plan-then-execute setup: the AI proposes an experiment plan and then, with confirmation cards, executes changes such as adding instrumentation. This study shows that letting people review the plan is not enough. Plausible-looking plans get approved even when wrong, and editing a good plan can make it worse. This matters for both the RQ1 hypothesis card and the assistant's confirmation flow.

## What you can take from it
- Do not assume that "editable" means "checked." Pair the editable hypothesis with prompts that point at where the plan is most likely wrong, such as the risk cards or a mismatch between changed screens and instrumented events.
- Borrow the design of planting flawed plans. Seed one wrong inference (for example, a primary metric that does not match the changed step) in the Phase 1 condition and record whether participants catch it, alongside restatement accuracy.
- Separate the plan check from execution confirmations. The paper found different effects for each, so measure them separately rather than as one "trust" score.
- Use it to argue that blind trust is a real risk, not hypothetical: participants accepted imperfect plans on the very tasks where it mattered most.

## Limits for your use
- Crowdworkers on everyday tasks, not designers judging experiment plans, and an older model (GPT-3.5).
- Large between-subjects N, which your 5 to 8 person study cannot replicate; borrow the trap idea, not the statistics.
