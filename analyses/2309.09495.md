# PwR: Exploring the Role of Representations in Conversational Programming

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · RQ1 Planning · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
YM et al. built PwR, a web tool where users build chatbots by talking to a GPT-4 "dev-bot" while a center pane shows the system's current understanding as editable natural-language representations (knowledge base, logic rules, variables), next to a live test-bot. 14 internal participants (3 novice, 6 intermediate, 5 expert programmers) built a quiz bot and a knowledge bot in lab sessions, followed by a focus group, with logs and NASA-TLX.

## Key findings
- 33 bots built; 214 dev-bot messages, 424 test-bot messages and 42 direct representation edits.
- 12 of 14 said representations improved understandability and gave a sense of agency; 12 of 14 reported a minimal learning curve (reported as opinion; the body gives no formal test for understandability).
- Experts read representations to verify and debug; intermediates glanced to confirm "something got edited"; novices largely ignored them.
- Novices found terms like "logic" and "variables" confusing; most avoided direct edits because the text felt too formal.
- Proposed improvements: show diffs after each update, plainer labels, guided onboarding, a mode to ask about state without changing it.

## Why it relates to your idea
PwR is the closest analogue to your RQ1 hypothesis card: the AI's inference is shown back in plain language beside a working, testable artifact, so the user can catch misunderstandings. Your design adds a live prototype with firing events in place of the test-bot.

## What you can take from it
- Expect your non-technical users to behave like PwR's intermediates and novices: they glance rather than verify. Make the card ask for an action (confirm, edit or reject each inferred item) rather than relying on reading.
- Avoid technical labels; PwR's own fix was "information library" instead of "knowledge base". Say "what we count" rather than "events", "the number that decides" rather than "primary metric".
- Show what changed when the assistant rewrites the brief (a diff), as PwR's participants requested.
- Offer an ask-only mode in your assistant that answers questions about the brief without writing to it, separating reading from your confirmation-gated writes.
- In RQ1 analysis, report whether people verified or just confirmed, using PwR's verify/confirm distinction.

## Limits for your use
- Programmers and STEM staff, not designers; N = 14.
- Building bots differs from judging an experiment plan.
