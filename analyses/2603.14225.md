# "I'm Not Reading All of That": Understanding Software Engineers' Level of Cognitive Engagement with Agentic Coding Assistants

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · Verifying AI analysis · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
A formative workshop study with N = 4 software engineers from one company in the Philippines, one at each experience level (under 1 year to over 10 years). Each used Cline, an agentic coding assistant in VS Code with a Plan mode followed by an Act mode, to generate an Excel-processing script. Right after the task they completed a survey built on Bloom's Taxonomy that tested recall, understanding, analysis and evaluation. The authors also ran a thematic analysis of the answers and the moderator's notes.

## Key findings
- Engagement peaked during planning, fell during execution and was minimal during evaluation. One senior participant said, "I'm not reading all of that."
- Participants judged success by output, not process: "It worked," "I trust Cline."
- Checking was "greedy": people chose the cheapest check that confirmed completion and went deeper only when it failed.
- Comprehension was shallow. None of the 4 correctly stated how many functions the script had. Two of 4 recalled the folder name and 3 of 4 the first file name.
- The authors propose (but did not test) visualizing plans as flowcharts and using cognitive-forcing designs that slow the agent or ask users to think before seeing output.

## Why it relates to your idea
Your AI assistant can open a branch, add instrumentation and submit a PR. This paper shows that even professional engineers skim agent output and trust it once it "works." Your users are less technical, so the risk is higher. It supports your rule that every write stops at a confirmation a non-technical person can read, and it warns that a confirmation card full of text may simply be skipped.

## What you can take from it
- Make confirmation cards short and visual. For example, show "adds a `checkout_step2_viewed` event to step 2" with the step highlighted on the prototype, not a diff or a paragraph. The paper links long text output to skimming.
- Put the confirmation at the planning moment, where engagement was highest, not after execution, where it was lowest.
- Borrow the Bloom-style recall probe for your comprehension test. After the session, ask what the AI changed (which event, which step) and score it, just as they asked how many functions the script had.
- The "greedy verification" finding is a good argument for your Phase 2 planted trap. People stop at the first check that looks fine, so the trap tests whether your results view pushes them one step further.

## Limits for your use
- N = 4, one closed task, self-report only, workshop paper. Treat it as a motivating observation, not evidence.
- Participants were engineers reading code, not designers reading an experiment brief.
