# Do You See What I See? A Qualitative Study Eliciting High-Level Visualization Comprehension

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · RQ2 Communicating · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
A qualitative study (CHI 2024) with N = 24 university-affiliated adults (19 students, 5 professionals). Each saw 12 of 60 graphs rebuilt from data journalism and government sources, with titles and captions removed, spanning scatter, line and bar charts, single- or multi-class data and juxtaposed or not. For each graph participants answered two open prompts: "Describe what do you see in the graph" and "What questions would you be able to answer from this graph?" Two coders (κ = 0.81) scored 288 responses against the designer's stated objective as a complete, general, partial or no match.

## Key findings
- Only 41% of responses (117 of 288) fully matched what the designer intended.
- Individual differences were large. Ten participants produced 77% of the complete matches; three people matched 11 of 12 graphs and three matched none.
- The question prompt aligned with intent more often than the description prompt (182 vs 117 of 288, though the authors note a mismatch with their table).
- Composition changed what people saw. A single-class scatterplot elicited correlation 90% of the time, a juxtaposed one 33%.
- Sixteen participants asked for more context, such as units or definitions.

## Why it relates to your idea
Your evaluation asks whether people can restate the hypothesis, primary metric and one risk after seeing a conventional brief or your page. This paper is a ready-made method for that: an open-ended prompt, a stated objective written in advance, and a graded match scale. It also shows that the gap between what an author intends and what a reader takes away is large even for simple charts, which supports RQ2.

## What you can take from it
- Write the "stated objective" for each brief before the study (the true hypothesis, primary metric and key risk), then code restatements as complete, general, partial or no match rather than right or wrong. A four-level scale works better with 5 to 8 people.
- Use both prompt types. "Describe what this experiment tests" and "What questions could this experiment answer?" draw out different levels of understanding.
- Report per-person results, not only averages, since this paper shows a few people can drive most of the correct answers.
- Have a second coder score a subset and report agreement, as the authors did, to make a small study more credible.
- Cite the 41% figure to argue that author-intended meaning often fails to reach the audience, which is the problem your interactive brief addresses.

## Limits for your use
- Static charts without titles, not experiment briefs, and a campus sample with no control for graph literacy.
- Purely qualitative, with no comparison of two presentation formats like your doc-versus-page design.
