# Tempo: Helping Data Scientists and Domain Experts Collaboratively Specify Predictive Modeling Tasks

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · RQ1 Planning · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
The authors built Tempo, a system that turns the specification of a temporal predictive model (what to predict, when, and for whom) into a readable query language with live previews. They evaluated it qualitatively through three case studies: a browser-extension start-up (an ML engineer and a product manager), ICU sepsis prediction (four hospital experts, about 14,000 patients) and home-health readmission (pharmacy experts, about 7,537 patients). The authors did most of the prototyping and gathered feedback in interviews. There was no controlled comparison and no logs.

## Key findings
- Domain experts critiqued the models through editable, rule-based subgroups rather than code or feature-importance plots. One expert said the rules were "much better than a variable importance plot."
- Automatic mis-specification alerts flagged "trivial" models (top 5 features reach at least 95% of full performance) and rare classes (recall under 10%). In case 3, an alert flagged heart failure, renal disease and cancer, which a pharmacist called "completely unsurprising."
- These critiques changed the specification. In the sepsis case, experts excluded patients given vasopressors in the prior 8 hours. In case 1, a refined target reached AUROC 0.83 and the product manager found a 1 to 5 hour target more useful. In case 3, the team pivoted after a renal subgroup reached only AUROC 0.51.
- The authors warn that LLM-generated queries risk subtle mis-specification, so readability and precision both matter.

## Why it relates to your idea
Tempo is the closest analogue to RQ1. It takes a technical specification that only experts usually read and makes it something a non-technical stakeholder (including a PM in case 1) can inspect and push back on before the team commits. Your hypothesis card, primary metric and guardrail markers play the same role for an experiment, and your risk cards are a close match for Tempo's mis-specification alerts.

## What you can take from it
- Model your risk cards on Tempo's alerts: each one fires on a concrete, checkable condition (for example, "this change only affects new users, so the sample is X% of traffic") rather than generic advice.
- Show the specification as a few structured slots (change, hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, duration), like Tempo's four editor sections, so people can critique one slot at a time.
- Let people critique through concrete cases, not abstractions. Tempo's experts used subgroups; your equivalent is clicking the prototype and watching which events fire.
- Cite the authors' warning about LLM mis-specification as a reason the AI's inferred hypothesis must stay editable and visible.

## Limits for your use
- Data scientists did the authoring; domain experts mostly reviewed. Your users must also add their own intent.
- Three qualitative case studies and no comparison condition, so there is no effect size to cite.
