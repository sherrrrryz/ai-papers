# How Do Data Analysts Respond to AI Assistance? A Wizard-of-Oz Study

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · Verifying AI analysis · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
Gu et al. first reviewed 15 publications and the work of 29 analyst teams from a crowd-sourced soccer analysis to build categories of planning suggestions (domain background, operationalizing constructs, model choice, interpretation, high-level planning, execution). They then ran a Wizard-of-Oz lab study with 13 analysts (professors, PhD and master's students, a data scientist), all self-rated at least 4/5 in programming and statistics. A hidden human wizard pushed suggestion cards into a JupyterLab side panel during about 75 minutes of analysis.

## Key findings
- Analysts saw on average 11.85 suggestions (SD 4.56), 9.85 of them about planning; they integrated 51.6% (47 of 91) of the planning suggestions they could reasonably use.
- 12 of 13 said planning suggestions raised ideas they had not considered; 11 of 13 found the assistant helpful for both planning and execution.
- Helpfulness depended on context: suggestions felt too basic to statistical experts and too unfamiliar to others, and 9 of 13 felt some "unhelpful" suggestions would have helped at a different moment.
- Preferences for initiative varied (proactive vs. on-demand), and one analyst described becoming a "clicking machine" rather than a thinker.
- The authors give seven guidelines, including aligning goals between analyst and assistant, tailoring content to background, and reducing overreliance by promoting engagement.

## Why it relates to your idea
This is the closest prior work to your RQ1: AI support for *planning* an analysis rather than writing code. Your hypothesis card, primary-metric choice and risk cards are planning suggestions in their categories (operationalizing constructs, choosing what to measure). The difference is that their users were statistically proficient, while yours are not.

## What you can take from it
- Use their suggestion taxonomy to check coverage of your generated brief: your hypothesis maps to conceptual model formulation, events and metrics to operationalizing constructs, risk cards to interpretation threats.
- Their finding that fit to background drives helpfulness argues for writing risk cards in product language (one sentence, no jargon), which your proposal already plans.
- The "clicking machine" quote and guideline 6 (reduce overreliance via engagement) support making the hypothesis editable and asking users to add their own intent before accepting.
- Cite the selection criterion (proficiency at least 4/5) as the gap: planning assistance was studied only with experts, never with designers or PMs.
- Timing matters: consider showing risk cards after the user has looked at the diff, not upfront.

## Limits for your use
- Participants were expert analysts with programming and statistics skill, the opposite of your population.
- N = 13, one dataset, single session; the study measured reactions, not the quality of resulting analyses.
