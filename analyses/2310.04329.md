# Pika: Empowering Non-Programmers to Author Executable Governance Policies in Online Communities

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · RQ1 Planning · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
The authors built Pika, which lets people without programming skills write governance policies (for example, "a private-channel invite needs a consensus vote") that run directly on Slack. It combines a declarative language, a library of reusable policy components and a four-step form-based web interface that compiles to PolicyKit Python code. The user studies had 17 participants: 10 non-programmers with community governance experience and 7 programmers, who also wrote a policy in code as a baseline.

## Key findings
- Non-programmers completed 9 of 10 attempts at Task 1 and 7 of 9 at Task 2, averaging 7.5 and 8.6 minutes when successful. The paper reports this as about 2.5 times faster than programmers writing code.
- Programmers took 3.6 and 4.4 minutes in Pika versus 19.9 and 21.6 minutes in PolicyKit code (p < 0.01). Pika's SUS was 75.85 versus 35.45 for code.
- Non-programmers struggled most with command-triggered policies, especially how command parameters become election candidates, and several chose the wrong filter first.
- 4 of 10 participants spent half their authoring time refining notification messages, the part closest to their own intent.
- The interface had no preview or simulation of what a policy would govern; the authors list real-time feedback as future work.

## Why it relates to your idea
Pika shows that non-programmers can produce something executable if the specification is broken into constrained components with drop-downs populated from their real context. This is a pattern for RQ1 where a designer adds intent the AI missed, for example "also track the coupon field," and for how the assistant turns that into an instrumentation PR.

## What you can take from it
- When a designer adds an event or metric, offer choices drawn from the real prototype (steps, buttons, fields) instead of free text, the way Pika fills Slack channels into drop-downs.
- Your live event panel fills the gap Pika names: it lets people see what their specification does by clicking through the prototype. You can position it as that missing preview.
- Watch for the abstraction Pika's users found hardest, the mapping from a trigger to a parameter. In your case this is the link from an event to the metric it feeds, so show it explicitly.
- Expect people to spend effort on wording they own. Keep the hypothesis sentence editable even when the rest is generated.

## Limits for your use
- Governance policies on Slack, not product experiments, and the users had community governance experience.
- Small lab sessions (17 people), and the programmer baseline was simplified.
