# From Teacher to Colleague: How Coding Experience Shapes Developer Perceptions of AI Tools

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · Verifying AI analysis · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
Zakharov, Koshchenko and Sergeyuk ran a secondary analysis of JetBrains' State of Developer Ecosystem 2024 survey. From 19,820 developers who code at work, 8,073 answered the AI awareness and experience questions and 3,380 chose at least one role for AI. They used Spearman correlations and per-role logistic regressions with false discovery rate correction.

## Key findings
- Coding experience did not correlate with AI experience (rho = -0.005, p = 0.652) and only negligibly with AI awareness (rho = 0.031).
- More experienced developers were more likely to see AI as a junior colleague (OR 1.170), a content generator (OR 1.087), or to assign it no role (OR 1.380).
- Less experienced developers were more likely to see AI as a teacher (OR 0.883 per unit of experience).
- The authors conclude AI tools should match users' expertise and mental models: experts need evidence of concrete value, novices benefit from learning-oriented features.

## Why it relates to your idea
Your proposal rests on the claim that expertise shapes how people relate to AI output ("operations got easier; judgment did not"). This paper shows the same pattern for developers: adoption is flat across experience, but the role people give AI differs. Your designers and PMs are novices in code and statistics, so they are likely to treat the AI assistant as a teacher, which is exactly the trust posture your confirmation cards and planted traps are meant to probe.

## What you can take from it
- A citation for the motivation: experience does not predict who uses AI, but it does predict how they frame it, which supports "everyone can use the tool; not everyone can judge it."
- A design caution: if non-technical users see the AI as a teacher, phrase the hypothesis card and risk cards as claims to check, not lessons to absorb.
- A cheap measure for your study: ask participants which role they see the assistant playing (teacher, junior colleague, generator, none) using the same fixed options, and relate it to who fell for the planted trap.
- The "teacher" framing also suggests a positive use: confirmation cards can explain what a write (branch, PR) does, building understanding rather than just asking for a click.

## Limits for your use
- Population is software developers, measured on coding experience, not designers or statistical knowledge.
- Workshop paper, correlational survey with self-selection (3,380 of 19,820 answered the role question) and predefined role options.
