# Practitioners' Perspectives on Designing Data Visualizations for the General Public

*Analysis for your experiment-brief proposal · RQ3 Reading results · based on the full text*

## What the paper did
Semi-structured interviews (about one hour each, December 2022 to June 2023) with N = 21 visualization practitioners from newsrooms, graphics agencies, freelancing, a statistical office and science communication groups in 10 countries, mostly Germany and Austria. Each brought a visualization they had made to anchor the discussion. The authors used reflexive thematic analysis.

## Key findings
- Practitioners considered knowing the audience essential but usually described it vaguely as "the general public." The distribution channel shaped design more than any audience model.
- They worked toward four aims: attracting attention, supporting comprehension, building credibility and creating emotional connection.
- Simplification was the default for audiences assumed to have low attention or literacy. Common tactics were one clear message (often in the title), highlighting, stepwise reveals and familiar charts (bar, line, maps). Pie charts, scatter plots, dual axes and log scales were usually avoided.
- Views on whether to show uncertainty were divided.
- Formal audience testing was rare. Practitioners relied on hallway feedback and editors as stand-ins for readers, and saw clicks and dwell time as measures of attention, not comprehension.

## Why it relates to your idea
Your Phase 2 results view is a chart made by experts for readers without statistics, which is the same situation these practitioners face. Their tactics map closely onto your design (conclusion first, a few fixed chart templates, the funnel shown on the prototype). Their split on uncertainty is exactly the open question in RQ3.

## What you can take from it
- Use "message-first" as support for your conclusion-first results view. The authors recommend workflows that set the takeaway before choosing a chart, which is what your model-written caption above a fixed template does.
- Limit your four chart templates to familiar forms (bars, lines, a funnel) and avoid dual axes or log scales, following professional practice for lay readers.
- Cite the divided views on uncertainty to show RQ3 is unresolved in practice, not only in research. Your comprehension test with a planted trap gives evidence that practitioners say they lack.
- Cite the lack of formal testing as a gap. Your evaluation measures comprehension directly instead of relying on proxies like clicks.
- Watch the risk: simplification can hide nuance. Your "segment flagged when sample too small" marker is a way to simplify without hiding the caveat.

## Limits for your use
- Public-facing news and communication graphics, not internal product dashboards read by colleagues.
- Self-reported practices and beliefs about audiences, from a mostly German-speaking sample.
